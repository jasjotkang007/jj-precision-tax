SAVE & PLAN AHEAD

RRSP & FHSA Guide

Registered savings plans can affect your tax return differently. Review contribution room and eligibility before contributing; your CRA account and notices are important sources for your personal limits.

RRSP — Retirement Savings

Eligible RRSP contributions may be deducted from taxable income, subject to your deduction limit. You may be able to claim a deduction in a later year rather than the year you contribute. Withdrawals are generally taxable, subject to specific exceptions such as qualifying Home Buyers’ Plan or Lifelong Learning Plan withdrawals.

Before contributing: check your RRSP deduction limit, consider employer pension adjustments and unused contributions, and keep contribution receipts. Contributions made in the first 60 days of a year may be eligible for the previous tax year, but confirm the CRA deadline for that year.

Read CRA RRSP guidance ↗

FHSA — First Home Savings Account

An FHSA can help eligible first-time home buyers save for a qualifying home. Eligible contributions may be deducted, and qualifying withdrawals to buy a first home can be tax-free. The annual participation limit is generally $8,000, with a $40,000 lifetime limit, subject to carry-forward and other rules.

Before contributing: check eligibility and your participation room. Your room starts in the year you open your first FHSA, and RRSP-to-FHSA transfers count toward the FHSA room but generally do not create an FHSA deduction.

Read CRA FHSA guidance ↗

Read the detailed RRSP & FHSA guide →

Planning tip: RRSP and FHSA deductions may be claimed in different years depending on the rules, but you should not contribute beyond your available room. Check your Notice of Assessment or CRA account, and seek advice if you are unsure which account best fits your income, home-buying plans or retirement goals.

Year-End Tax-Planning Checklist

A practical reminder list to help you gather records and identify questions before December 31. Not every item applies to everyone.

Personal & family

  • Check RRSP deduction limit and unused contributions.
  • If eligible, review FHSA room and first-home plans before year-end.
  • Check TFSA room before contributing or withdrawing.
  • Gather charitable donation and eligible medical-expense receipts.
  • Review tuition slips, unused tuition amounts and education-related records.
  • Update CRA about marital status, address and family changes where required.

Self-employed & business owners

  • Reconcile income, bank deposits, invoices and expenses.
  • Collect receipts and document the business purpose of expenses.
  • Update vehicle kilometre logs and business-use calculations.
  • Review GST/HST, payroll remittances and instalment obligations.
  • Review major equipment purchases and whether capital-cost rules apply.
  • For corporations, confirm year-end, T2, annual filing and balance-due dates.

STAY ORGANIZED

CRA Accounts & Recordkeeping

Good records can make tax filing easier and help you respond if the CRA requests supporting documents.

Keep key tax documents

  • Notices of assessment and reassessment.
  • T4, T5, T3, T2202 and other tax slips.
  • RRSP and FHSA contribution receipts and transfer records.
  • Donation, eligible medical and childcare receipts where relevant.
  • Business invoices, receipts, bank statements, mileage logs and GST/HST records.
  • CRA letters, payment confirmations and supporting schedules.

Keep your information current

  • Check your CRA account and notices for room, balances and deadlines.
  • Update address, direct deposit and relevant family information when it changes.
  • For business accounts, track GST/HST and payroll filing and remittance dates separately.
  • Use secure sign-in practices and never share passwords or multi-factor authentication codes.
  • Retain records for the applicable period; business and special circumstances can affect how long records must be kept.

Common Tax Questions

Select a question to read more. These answers provide general guidance; exceptions may apply.

Personal Tax

When do I need to file my personal income tax return?

Most individuals file by April 30 of the year after the tax year. Self-employed individuals generally have until June 15 to file, but any balance owing is generally due April 30. Special rules can apply in certain situations.

Filing may be important even if you had little or no income. Your return may be needed to calculate or continue benefits and credits, and filing may allow you to receive a refund if tax was deducted from your income. See CRA filing dates ↗

Can I claim expenses when working from home?

It depends on whether you are an employee or self-employed. Employees must meet CRA eligibility conditions for employment-use-of-home expenses. Simply working remotely does not automatically make you eligible.

Self-employed individuals may generally deduct a reasonable business-use portion of eligible expenses, such as utilities, rent or maintenance, when the relevant conditions are met. Keep receipts and records of how you calculated the business-use portion. Personal expenses and mortgage principal are not deductible simply because you work from home.

What happens if I owe money to the CRA and cannot pay it all at once?

Check your notice of assessment and CRA account to confirm the amount and deadline. Interest may apply to an unpaid balance after the due date.

If you cannot pay in full, contact the CRA to discuss payment arrangements. Interest may continue to accrue. If you think the assessment is incorrect, options to request a correction or file an objection may be available, depending on the issue and deadline.

Why should I file a return if I have little or no income?

Filing can help the CRA determine whether you qualify for benefits or credits and may be necessary to continue receiving payments. It may also allow you to claim a refund if tax was deducted, report eligible tuition amounts, or preserve certain unused credits. Whether filing is required depends on your circumstances.

Registered Savings & Tax Planning

Does an RRSP contribution always have to be deducted in the same year?

No. An eligible contribution can generally be reported and the deduction may be carried forward to a later year, subject to the rules and your available deduction limit. A contribution and a deduction are not the same thing. Check your RRSP deduction limit and report contributions accurately, including amounts not deducted in prior years.

RRSP withdrawals are generally taxable. Special programs such as the Home Buyers’ Plan have eligibility and repayment rules. See CRA RRSP guidance ↗

What is the difference between an RRSP and an FHSA?

An RRSP is primarily designed for retirement savings. Eligible contributions may reduce taxable income, while withdrawals are generally taxable. An FHSA is intended for eligible first-time home buyers: eligible contributions may be deductible, and qualifying withdrawals for a first home can be tax-free.

FHSA eligibility, contribution room and qualifying withdrawal conditions are specific. RRSP-to-FHSA transfers use FHSA room and generally do not produce a new FHSA deduction. Review your circumstances before choosing an account. See CRA FHSA guidance ↗

Can I contribute to an FHSA after December 31 for the previous tax year?

Generally, no. FHSA contributions are counted in the calendar year they are made; the RRSP first-60-days rule does not apply to FHSAs. Check your available participation room before contributing, including any amounts transferred from an RRSP.

Does a TFSA contribution reduce my income tax?

No. TFSA contributions are not deductible, but eligible investment income and withdrawals are generally tax-free. Withdrawn contribution room is generally added back on January 1 of the following calendar year, not immediately, so recontributing too soon can create an excess amount.

Always verify your TFSA room and recent transactions before contributing. Read CRA TFSA guidance ↗

Should I prioritize an RRSP, FHSA or TFSA?

There is no single best order for everyone. An FHSA may be especially relevant if you are eligible and saving for a qualifying first home. RRSP contributions may be useful when the deduction is valuable and retirement saving is a priority. TFSA contributions do not generally create a deduction, but eligible withdrawals are tax-free and do not add to taxable income.

Compare eligibility, time horizon, current and expected future tax rates, employer pension matching, liquidity needs and contribution room. This is a planning decision, not just a comparison of account limits.

Small Business & Self-Employment

When does a business need to register for GST/HST?

Many businesses must register when they stop being a small supplier under the GST/HST rules. The commonly referenced threshold is $30,000 in taxable supplies, but the timing of when it is exceeded affects when registration and charging obligations begin. Special rules can apply, including to associated persons and certain types of supplies.

Some businesses may register voluntarily before reaching the threshold. Registration can allow eligible businesses to claim Input Tax Credits, but it also creates responsibilities to charge, collect, report and remit GST/HST as applicable. Read CRA GST/HST guidance ↗

Can I deduct vehicle expenses used for my business?

If you use a vehicle to earn business income, you may be able to deduct the business portion of eligible expenses, such as fuel, insurance, repairs, maintenance and licence fees. Lease costs and capital cost allowance for a purchased vehicle are subject to separate rules and limits.

Keep receipts and records of total kilometres and business kilometres. A logbook can support the business-use percentage. Ordinary travel between home and a regular workplace is generally personal commuting, not business travel.

What business expenses can I deduct?

Generally, reasonable expenses incurred to earn business income may be deductible when they meet the tax rules and are properly supported. Examples can include advertising, professional fees, office supplies, eligible software, insurance and certain travel costs.

Personal expenses are generally not deductible. Meals, mixed personal/business costs, vehicles and larger equipment purchases have special rules; some capital purchases must be deducted over time rather than in full immediately. Keep invoices, receipts and records explaining the business purpose.

Incorporation & Corporate Tax

Should I incorporate or operate as a sole proprietor?

A sole proprietorship is usually simpler and less expensive to set up and maintain. Business income is generally reported on the owner's personal tax return, and the owner is generally personally responsible for business obligations.

A corporation is a separate legal entity and may offer planning or liability advantages, but it also brings incorporation costs, annual filings, a separate tax return and additional recordkeeping. Incorporation does not eliminate every type of personal liability or automatically save tax. The right choice depends on profit, personal cash needs, business risks and future plans.

What is the difference between business registration and a Business Number?

Business registration or incorporation is a legal or registry process that depends on the business structure and jurisdiction. A CRA Business Number (BN) is a nine-digit identifier used in dealings with the CRA. A business may also need program accounts for corporate income tax, GST/HST or payroll.

Obtaining a BN does not by itself mean a business name has been registered or a corporation has been incorporated. The required steps depend on the business structure and location.

What happens if my corporation has been dissolved?

The options depend on the jurisdiction, why the corporation was dissolved and its circumstances. Revival or restoration may be available through the relevant corporate registry, but requirements, fees and deadlines vary.

Dissolution does not automatically clear outstanding CRA obligations. There may still be tax returns, balances, information filings or other matters to address. Confirm the corporation's status and the available options before deciding how to proceed.

Benefits & Family Tax

What is the Canada Child Benefit, and who may qualify?

The Canada Child Benefit is a tax-free monthly payment for eligible families raising children under 18. Eligibility and amounts depend on factors such as adjusted family net income, the number and ages of eligible children, and family circumstances. Shared custody and changes in marital status can affect payments.

Eligible individuals generally need to file annual tax returns, and their spouse or common-law partner generally needs to file as well. Keep CRA information current when circumstances change.
