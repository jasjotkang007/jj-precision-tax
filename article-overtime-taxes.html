Is Working Overtime Worth It?
Understand the tax effect.

Overtime can increase your tax bill, but that does not usually mean you are worse off for earning more. The key is to compare the extra income with the additional tax and payroll deductions.

Reviewed: October 2026 · General Canadian information

Why overtime can look heavily taxed

Canada uses a progressive income tax system. As taxable income rises, portions of income can be taxed at higher marginal rates. Your entire salary does not suddenly become taxed at the highest rate just because you worked overtime.

Simple example

Imagine you earn $90,000 in regular employment income and receive another $20,000 in overtime. Your total employment income becomes $110,000. You will generally owe more income tax than if you earned only $90,000, but the additional $20,000 is still additional income. The amount you keep depends on federal and provincial/territorial tax, deductions, credits and payroll contributions.

This illustration is conceptual, not a calculation of a particular province's tax or an exact take-home amount.

What can affect the amount you keep?

  • Your province or territory of residence for tax purposes.
  • Your total annual income from all jobs and other sources.
  • Income tax withheld by each employer and your credits or deductions.
  • CPP/QPP contributions and EI premiums. Multiple employers may each withhold these amounts, and eligible overpayments may be reconciled on your tax return, subject to the rules.
  • Whether overtime affects income-tested benefits or other tax-related amounts.

The takeaway

Higher marginal tax does not normally make earning additional overtime pay financially pointless. The useful question is how much extra income you may keep, and whether the hours are worth it for you beyond tax alone.
