FEDERAL BUDGET & TAX UPDATES

Filing Deadline Change,
CGEB Supplement and Other Tax Changes

Budget 2026 includes several proposed changes that could affect personal tax filing, benefit payments, retirement savings, foreign property reporting and tax instalments.

Published November 6, 2026 · JJ Precision Accounting & Tax Inc.

The federal government tabled Budget 2026 on November 5, 2026. Alongside broader economic measures, it announced a number of tax and benefit changes that are particularly relevant to individuals, families, retirees and self-employed Canadians.

Here is a practical overview of the announcements, including when the proposed changes are expected to take effect.

Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit – One-Time Supplement

Budget 2026 proposes a one-time Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (CGEB) supplement to be delivered in early 2027. The supplement would equal half of the maximum annual CGEB amounts for the 2026–27 benefit year.

  • Up to $223 per adult.
  • Up to $117 per child.
  • An additional $117 single supplement for eligible single individuals.

The actual amount will depend on household circumstances and the benefit's income-tested rules. The supplement is intended as a one-time payment, separate from the regular quarterly CGEB payments.

Personal Tax Filing and Payment Deadline Moving to May 15

Budget 2026 proposes a common May 15 filing and balance-due date for individual income tax returns, including returns for self-employed individuals and their spouses or common-law partners.

Currently, most individuals must file and pay any balance owing by April 30. Self-employed individuals and their spouses or common-law partners generally have until June 15 to file, but any balance owing is still due April 30.

Proposed effective date: The May 15 deadline would apply to the 2027 and subsequent taxation years. The first affected personal return would be the 2027 tax return, filed in 2028, if the proposal is enacted.

For self-employed taxpayers, this would mean an earlier filing deadline than the current June 15 date, while also moving the payment deadline later from April 30 to May 15.

More Time to File a Notice of Objection

Taxpayers who disagree with a CRA assessment or reassessment can generally challenge it by filing a Notice of Objection. The current rules generally provide 90 days, with additional timing rules for individuals and graduated rate estates and a process to request an extension.

Budget 2026 proposes that most taxpayers would have one year after the Notice of Assessment or Reassessment is sent to file an objection. For individuals and graduated rate estates, the deadline would be the later of:

  • One year after the assessment or reassessment is sent; or
  • Two years after the filing due date for the tax year being assessed.

The proposal would also remove the separate extension-request process. It would apply to assessments sent on or after the day that is three months after the legislation receives Royal Assent.

RRSP for Life – Proposed Retirement Account Changes

Under the current rules, an individual must generally mature their RRSP by the end of the year they turn 71. Common options include converting it to a Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF), purchasing an eligible annuity or withdrawing the funds.

Budget 2026 announces the government's intention to combine core RRSP and RRIF features into a single arrangement referred to as “RRSP for life.” The intention is to remove the need to convert an RRSP to a RRIF at age 71. Minimum withdrawal requirements would continue to apply after age 71.

This is not yet a finalized change. The government is consulting on how features such as a successor holder for a spouse or common-law partner and tax-deferred transfers to qualifying survivors could work. The Department of Finance is accepting written submissions until January 8, 2027.

Higher Foreign Property Reporting Threshold – Form T1135

Canadian residents who own specified foreign property may have to file Form T1135, Foreign Income Verification Statement. Currently, the filing requirement generally applies when the total cost amount of specified foreign property exceeds $100,000 at any time during the year.

Budget 2026 proposes increasing the general filing threshold to $200,000. It also proposes increasing the upper limit for using the simplified reporting method from less than $250,000 to less than $500,000.

Reporting thresholdCurrentProposed
General T1135 filing thresholdMore than $100,000More than $200,000
Simplified reporting methodLess than $250,000Less than $500,000

The proposed changes would apply to taxation years or fiscal periods ending after 2026. The test is based on the cost amount of specified foreign property, not simply its current market value. Not every foreign asset is reportable, and the exclusions and reporting rules still matter.

Changes to Tax Instalment Requirements

Individuals who receive income without sufficient tax withheld at source may have to pay income tax in quarterly instalments. Under the current general rules, instalments are generally required when the relevant net tax owing exceeds $3,000 for the current year and either of the two preceding years. For individuals resident in Quebec, the federal threshold is $1,800.

Budget 2026 proposes increasing the thresholds as follows:

TaxpayerCurrent thresholdProposed threshold
Individuals outside Quebec$3,000$7,500
Individuals resident in Quebec$1,800$4,500
Corporations – federal tax$3,000$7,500

The proposed thresholds would be indexed to inflation and rounded to the nearest $500. The change would apply to taxation years beginning on or after January 1, 2028. As under the current rules, the threshold would need to be exceeded in the current year and either of the two previous years.

The budget also proposes allowing individuals whose primary source of income is business income, including professional or commission income, to make one annual instalment instead of quarterly payments. The annual instalment would generally equal two-thirds of the individual's combined federal-provincial net tax owing for the year and would be due by December 31. The remaining balance would be due on the applicable balance-due date. This option is proposed for the 2028 and subsequent taxation years.

What Should Taxpayers Do Now?

These announcements are budget proposals and some require legislative changes or further administrative guidance. Until the changes are enacted and their effective dates are confirmed, taxpayers should continue following the existing filing deadlines, instalment requirements and foreign property reporting rules.

  • Continue using the current filing and payment deadlines for the 2026 tax year unless legislation confirms otherwise.
  • Continue making instalment payments when required under the current rules.
  • Continue filing Form T1135 when currently required.
  • Keep copies of CRA assessments and supporting documents if you may need to dispute an assessment.
  • Do not assume the proposed RRSP-for-life structure is already available.
